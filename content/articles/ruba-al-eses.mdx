Ruba spent years learning how to hide parts of herself.
Some of that started when she was still a child.
After losing part of one of her fingers in an accident, she became conscious of her hand from a very young age. She learned to fold it when she spoke, to move in ways that made it less visible, and to avoid drawing attention to it.
It became automatic.
So automatic that even as an adult, opening her hand fully can still feel uncomfortable.

“I got used to hiding it without even thinking.”

For years, that small habit carried a much bigger feeling behind it: the feeling of being different.
Then life added other layers.
Marriage. Motherhood. Leaving work. Health struggles. Weight gain. Comments from people around her.
Little by little, Ruba became less visible in her own life.
She stopped wanting to be photographed. She spent more time at home. She became the person taking family pictures instead of the person standing in them.
One day, looking through photos of her daughters, she realized just how absent she had become.

“I barely had pictures with my own daughters. I was always the one behind the camera.”

That moment stayed with her.
It was not only about weight anymore. It was about how far she had moved away from herself.
After health treatment and later gastric surgery, Ruba began to rebuild her confidence. She lost weight, started going out again, and slowly allowed herself back into the frame.
But the deeper change came when she decided to speak.
Finding Her Voice
Social media was not something Ruba wanted to do just for attention.
Her husband had encouraged her before, but she resisted.

“If I was going to do it, I wanted it to mean something.”

Then one evening, while speaking with a friend, she admitted how much she missed the person she used to be.
The lively Ruba.
The outspoken Ruba.
The one who never stayed quiet when something felt wrong.
The next morning, she turned on the camera and began talking.
She spoke about motherhood, bullying, family pressure, harassment, violence, and the things women often experience quietly.
Women began responding to her.
I thought I was the only one.
That mattered to her.
She wanted people to feel seen.
But being visible brought her back to the part of herself she had spent years trying to hide.
The Hand She Stopped Hiding
As Ruba became more comfortable on camera, she stopped thinking so much about how she positioned her hand.
Then the comments came.
Some people told her she should hide it.
Others said they felt uncomfortable seeing it.
One person even questioned how her daughters could hold her hand.
That comment affected her deeply.
Not only as a woman.
As a mother.
She thought about her daughters growing up and one day seeing those words.
And that became a turning point.
Instead of hiding her hand again, Ruba chose to speak about it openly.

“I wanted my daughters to see me and know there is nothing wrong with me.”

That moment was about far more than a finger.
It was about refusing to teach her daughters shame.
It was about showing them that being different does not make someone less worthy, less beautiful, or less deserving of being seen.
It was also about finally giving herself the same acceptance.
Words Stay
Ruba knows how long words can live inside someone.
A comment about weight.
A joke about appearance.
A cruel message under a video.
A sentence said casually that stays in someone’s mind for years.

“Words have weight. We need to choose them carefully.”

For Ruba, confidence did not arrive all at once.
It came slowly.
In family photos.
In speaking openly.
In moving her hands freely.
In no longer apologizing for the parts of herself other people once made her feel she should hide.

Today, people often tell her that she says what they are thinking.
That she speaks for things others are afraid to say.
But perhaps the most important thing Ruba has done is simpler than that.
She stopped disappearing.
She came back into the picture.
She found her voice again.
And she stopped hiding the hand she had spent most of her life trying to protect from other people’s eyes.

“I didn’t become someone new. I found my way back to myself.”
