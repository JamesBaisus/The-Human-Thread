'> 'There was a time when Ruba barely appeared in her own family photos.
She was always the one behind the camera, taking pictures of her daughters and her husband, but rarely stepping into the frame herself.
When she noticed it, it hurt.
“I realised I barely had pictures with my daughters,” she says. “I was always the one taking the photo.”
It wasn’t because she didn’t want the memories. She simply didn’t feel comfortable being seen.
Motherhood had changed her life. She had left work to care for her daughter, spent more time at home, and slowly became disconnected from the energetic, outspoken woman she remembered being.
Then came the comments about her weight.
Some were jokes. Some came from people close to her. Some compared her constantly to how she looked before marriage and motherhood.
Eventually, those words began to stay with her.
“I reached a point where I didn’t like myself.”

Her weight became part of a much bigger emotional struggle. After health problems, treatment, and later gastric surgery, Ruba began losing weight and rebuilding her confidence.
But looking back, she knows the real change was not simply physical.
She wanted her life back.
She wanted to go out again. To take pictures with her daughters. To feel present rather than watching her own life from the side.
And slowly, she did.
Finding Her Voice
The idea of social media had been around for a while. Her husband had encouraged her to try it, even suggesting she film the meals she cooked at home.
But Ruba knew that wasn’t what she wanted.
“If I was going to be on social media, I wanted there to be a reason.”
One evening, while talking to a close friend, she finally admitted how she felt.
She missed herself.
The friend reminded her of the Ruba she had always known — energetic, funny, outspoken and unafraid to defend people.
The next morning, Ruba dropped her daughters at school, got dressed, turned on the camera and started talking.
That was the beginning.
She began speaking about motherhood, bullying, family pressures, harassment, violence and the situations women often discuss privately but rarely say out loud.
And women responded.
They wrote to her saying:
I thought I was the only one.
For Ruba, that mattered.
She didn’t want to create content simply to be visible. She wanted people to feel less alone.
The Part She Used to Hide
There was another part of herself Ruba had spent years trying not to show.
As a child, she lost part of one of her fingers in an accident.
Growing up, she became used to hiding her hand. She kept it folded when she spoke so people wouldn’t notice.
It became so natural that she barely thought about it anymore.
Until social media.
People started commenting on her hand.
Some told her she shouldn’t show it. Others made cruel remarks about how it looked.
One comment even questioned how her daughters could hold her hand.
That one stayed with her.
She cried. She was hurt.
But then something changed.
Instead of hiding her hand again, she spoke about it.
“I wanted my daughters to grow up and see that there is nothing wrong with me.”

![](/articles/ruba-al-eses/01.jpg)

For Ruba, it became bigger than one comment.
It became about the things we say to people without knowing what they have already lived through.
Words Stay
That is perhaps the clearest message in Ruba’s story.
Words stay.
A joke about someone’s body can stay.
A comment about the way they look can stay.
A sentence written under a video can stay with someone long after the person who wrote it has forgotten it.
Ruba knows that because she lived it.
But she also knows that those words do not have to define the ending.
Today, she is visible again.
She takes pictures with her daughters.
She speaks with her hands.
She talks about the subjects she cares about.
And the woman she thought she had lost is not gone.
She was there all along.
“I didn’t become someone new. I found my way back to myself.”

The Human Thread Question
What would you say to someone who has spent years hiding a part of themselves because of what other people might think?
Perhaps Ruba’s story already gives us the answer:
You do not have to disappear to make other people comfortable.
