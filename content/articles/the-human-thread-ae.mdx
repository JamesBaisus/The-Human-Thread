RUBA AL-ESES: THE COURAGE TO BE SEEN
There are versions of ourselves we lose quietly.
For Ruba Al-Eses, that happened somewhere between marriage, motherhood, illness, body changes, and years of comments that slowly affected the way she saw herself.
She had once been energetic, outspoken and full of life. Then motherhood changed everything. She left work, became more isolated, and began hearing constant remarks about her weight and appearance.
Eventually, she stopped wanting to be photographed.
One day, looking through family pictures, she realised she had almost no photos with her daughters.
“I was always the one taking the picture. I realised I was disappearing from my own memories.”

That moment became one of her biggest motivations to change.
After treatment for health issues and later gastric surgery, Ruba lost weight and slowly began rebuilding her confidence. But the deeper transformation was not physical.
It was emotional.
“I cried because of the comments. Then I asked myself: why am I letting this break me?”

As her confidence returned, so did her desire to create something meaningful.
Ruba entered social media with a clear purpose. She wanted to speak about the subjects people often avoid: motherhood, bullying, harassment, violence, family pressure, and the emotional realities women carry quietly.
Her honesty quickly resonated.
Women began telling her, I thought I was the only one.
But visibility brought another challenge.
When Ruba was a child, she lost part of one of her fingers in an accident. For years, she learned to hide her hand. Even as an adult, that instinct stayed with her.
Then people online began commenting on it.
Some told her she should hide it. Others questioned how she could appear so confidently on camera.
One comment, involving her daughters, affected her deeply.
Instead of hiding again, Ruba chose to speak.
“I wanted my daughters to grow up knowing there is nothing wrong with me.”

![](/articles/the-human-thread-ae/01.jpg)

That moment became part of something bigger.
For Ruba, the lesson is simple: words matter.
They can stay with someone for years. They can shape confidence, create shame, or make someone disappear from their own life.
But they do not have to have the final word.
“I didn’t become someone new. I simply found my way back to myself.”

Today, Ruba’s voice is part of her strength.
She speaks for the subjects people often keep hidden, and in doing so, reminds others that confidence is not about perfection.
Sometimes, it is simply about choosing not to hide anymore.
THE HUMAN THREAD QUESTION
What would you say to someone who has spent years believing that something about them makes them less worthy of being seen?
Ruba’s message is clear:
“Do not let someone else’s words become the voice you use against yourself.”
