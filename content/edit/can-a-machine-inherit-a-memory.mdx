Aziza Kadyri brings centuries of women’s craftsmanship into conversation with artificial intelligence.

For generations, suzani embroidery has carried more than pattern. Traditionally created across Central Asia, often as part of a young woman’s dowry, every flower, stitch and symbol held traces of family, identity and the hands that came before.

Now, Uzbek artist Aziza Kadyri is asking what happens when that memory enters the digital age.

In Sender Unknown, presented at the V&A during London Design Festival 2026, Aziza Kadyri brings traditional embroidery into conversation with artificial intelligence, digital printing and contemporary installation.

![](/edit/can-a-machine-inherit-a-memory/01.jpg)

---

But beneath the technology lies a deeply human question.

Can a machine reproduce an image without inheriting the life behind it?

A computer can recognise a pattern. It can recreate colour, symmetry and form. But it cannot remember the woman who spent months stitching a textile by hand, the home in which it was made, or the hopes quietly woven into it.

That tension gives Aziza Kadyri’s work its power. Rather than positioning technology against tradition, she places them beside one another, allowing us to see what each can and cannot preserve.

![](/edit/can-a-machine-inherit-a-memory/02.jpg)

---

At a moment when images can be generated in seconds, Sender Unknown reminds us that craftsmanship has never simply been about the finished object.

It is about time. Touch. Knowledge. Memory.

![](/edit/can-a-machine-inherit-a-memory/03.jpg)

---

Perhaps the future of craft will include machines.

But its soul still belongs to the human hand.
